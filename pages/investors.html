The opportunity

Docks that don’t rot out from under South Florida

Miami-Dade has more registered boats than any other county in Florida: 76,585 in 2024, according to the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission. Every marina, dock, and seawall that serves them stands on piles.

The problem with treated-timber piles

  • Marine borers. Florida Sea Grant researchers found that pressure-treated lumber expected to last 20 to 30 years elsewhere can fail structurally in less than 10 years in Florida. Our water never gets cold enough to slow wood borers down.
  • Treatments that borers tolerate. In Florida waters, the gribble Limnoria tolerates standard creosote treatment, and the boring isopod Sphaeroma is quite tolerant of CCA (chromated copper arsenate) treatment.
  • Chemical leaching. NOAA Fisheries guidance notes that copper leaches from CCA- and ACZA-treated wood, mostly in the first days to months after installation, and that creosote is a coal-tar product made mostly of PAHs. NOAA says treated wood “cannot be considered categorically safe” in the water and requires project-by-project assessment.

Recycled plastic: a long-life alternative

Researchers at Rutgers’ plastics recycling program, who helped develop structural plastic lumber, wrote that recycled plastic lumber “resists marine borers, corrosion and does not leach toxic chemicals.” The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and Rutgers developed and demonstrated composite fender, load-bearing, and sheet piles for waterfront use, and ASTM now publishes a standard specification for polymeric piles (ASTM D7258).

To be clear about the engineering: plastic lumber is less stiff than timber and can creep under sustained load (U.S. EPA). Every pile design has to be engineered, tested, and permitted for its use. That’s why testing and permitting are a core part of this raise.
